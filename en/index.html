Introduction

Hello, my name is Eric Bernier. I am a PhD candidate in public health at the School of Public Health of the Université de Montréal, co-supervised by Dr. Nadia Sourial and Dr. Géraldine Layani.

My project is entitled: Organizing the intersectoral response to complex and co-occurring social needs in primary care. Configuring networks adapted to the different profiles of patients in care.

This version describes the poster in four parts: the context, the methods, the expected results, and the impacts and outcomes.

1. Context

First, social needs pile up. In Canadian primary care, one patient in two reports at least one social need (Kosowan et al., 2025). In Québec, 19% of households experienced food insecurity in 2023, up from 14% in 2018, and so did 40% of households spending at least half their income on housing: housing or food (Institut de la statistique du Québec, 2026).

The poster distinguishes three notions. Co-occurrence is the presence of at least two social needs at the same time: it is the inclusion criterion. Complexity is the accumulation of chronic diseases, disabilities, mental health problems and social vulnerability: it is described, not required. The intersectoral response refers to actions by sectors other than health, carried out with it, on health or its determinants: the CLSC, the hospital, family medicine groups, pharmacies, community organizations, the municipality, schools, childcare centres and private actors. In short, co-occurrence is counted, complexity is described, and the intersectoral response is organized within a territory.

At the centre of the poster, a figure from Québec's 2026 primary care policy, issued by the Ministry of Health and Social Services, shows local governance led by the CLSC of the territory. On the left, a map of Québec divided into CLSC territories. On the right, around a CLSC that serves as an affiliation setting for every person, are a childcare centre, a clinic, a community organization, the municipality, the hospital, a pharmacy and a school: the neighbourhood of care and services. The CLSC territory becomes the reference unit, and the policy gives the CLSC four roles: navigator, coordinator, intersectoral leader and co-facilitator, in a spirit of shared governance.

To follow the logic, the poster uses a fictional case: Ms. Protocol, aged 58. She has received an eviction notice, faces food insecurity, has debts, and lives with depression, which forms her clinical context. Who responds to her? With whom? And who coordinates?

This is the gap: the CLSC must coordinate, yet for a given combination of needs, nothing says which partners to mobilize, how to link them, or who should coordinate. The planned programming is known, but not the networks actually formed around care pathways.

Hence the research question: how is the intersectoral response organized for the different profiles of patients with complex and co-occurring social needs cared for in primary care? The aim is to link each needs profile to a configuration of partners and to its coordinator.

2. Methods

The setting: three contrasting CLSC territories in Lanaudière, Québec, a pioneering region in intersectoral action with its own intersectoral table. The contrasts are real: material deprivation affects half of the population in Matawinie, but 7% in Les Moulins and L'Assomption (CISSS de Lanaudière, 2024). The design has several phases, shown as a metro line with four stations.

First station, objective 1: which profiles? A scoping review starts from published studies, identifies recurring needs profiles, and turns them into four to six vignettes, that is, fictional situations validated by six to eight stakeholders.

Second station, objective 2: who mobilizes whom? The mapping unfolds from each CLSC. Several CLSC practitioners and managers answer the questionnaire and name their contacts; these contacts receive the questionnaire in turn, and so on. The questionnaire covers the vignettes, the potential network, and the interactions of the last twelve months, the real network. Ties between partners are therefore reported by the partners themselves.

Third station, still objective 2: why and how? Qualitative network analysis explains these interactions. Fifteen to twenty-five interviews use the map, with questions such as: why and how do you work together? Two to three real pathways per profile are reconstructed with the person and the practitioners who supported them.

Fourth station, objective 3: what to recommend? A deliberative workshop brings together fifteen to twenty people: patient partners and actors from primary care, community organizations, local governance, municipal and private sectors. It produces recommendations for each profile.

The last stop integrates everything into a joint display, one row per profile: the network, the explanations and the recommendations. The project lasts 32 months, from 2026 to 2029.

Ms. Protocol travels the whole line: her profile is found in the literature, she becomes a vignette, her real pathway is explained, her case is deliberated in the workshop, and we obtain guidance for her profile.

3. Expected results

Three fictional examples illustrate what we expect in each CLSC: the ego network changes with the profile. In each drawing, the CLSC is at the centre; the organizations of the territory are arranged in a circle and grouped by sector; those the CLSC mobilizes are highlighted and linked to it.

Profile A, housing-dominant, after an eviction notice: the CLSC mobilizes three partners, a municipal partner, a community organization and a family medicine group. They are all linked to each other, and the CLSC coordinates.

Profile B, Ms. Protocol: housing, food and debts. Seven partners, still linked to each other, and the CLSC coordinates.

Profile C: the same needs, plus depression. Eleven partners, but loosely linked: small isolated groups, and no designated coordinator.

Five hypotheses guide the analysis, each with its measure. One: the sectors mobilized change with the profile, measured by sector composition. Two: there is a common core of partners and a periphery specific to each profile. Three: the more partners there are, the less connected they are, measured by network size and density. Four: does the CLSC play its roles of navigator and coordinator, according to the designated coordinator? Five: with equal needs, does gender change the response, comparing female and male versions of the vignettes? Finally, what gap separates the potential network, described by the vignettes, from the real network of the last twelve months?

4. Impacts and outcomes

The thesis does not seek a universal ideal network, but guidance for each profile. The outcomes range from the person to the system.

For people: a response tailored to their combination of needs. For the CLSC and its partners: guidance on whom to mobilize, how to connect and who coordinates. For primary care policy: a baseline, and recommendations aligned with the CLSC's four roles. For research: a hypothesis linking the needs profile to the network, and a measure of the gap between potential and real networks. Results will be shared with the three territories, the review protocol is published on OSF, and articles will follow.
